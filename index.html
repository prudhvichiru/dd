The assessment

What actually happens

From the first phone call to the letter that reaches your GP, here is the whole thing, step by step. It takes a few weeks, not a few years.

Step one: the first call

One of our nurses rings you. It takes about twenty minutes and there is no test in it. They'll ask what you've noticed, when it started, and how it's affecting ordinary days. If someone else noticed first, they're welcome on the call.

At the end of it you'll know whether an assessment makes sense, what it will involve, and what it will cost. If it isn't right for you, we'll say so and point you somewhere better.

Step two: gathering the background

This is the part that usually takes months. We do it for you.

What we collect

Your GP's records, past test results, your medication list, and anything from other specialists you've seen. We ask your permission for each source.

What we ask you for

A short questionnaire about daily life, and the same one for a family member if you have one. Both matter, and they often say slightly different things.

Tests before the appointment

Usually blood tests at your local collection centre, and sometimes a scan. We arrange the referrals so you don't have to chase them.

Thinking and memory tests

Done with a clinician guiding you, not alone with a screen. There's nothing to revise for and nothing to fail.

Step three: the consultation

Available from mid-2027. Video consultations are not running yet. Everything on this page describes the service as it will operate when appointments open.

Around an hour by video with a geriatrician or old-age psychiatrist. Longer than most specialist appointments, because the history is the diagnosis.

Family are welcome, in the room with you or dialling in from somewhere else. If you'd rather part of the conversation happened without them, say so and we'll arrange it.

What you need: a device with a camera, somewhere quiet, and about 60 minutes free. If the technology is the obstacle, tell us and we'll sort it out before the day.

If video won't work at all, tell us that too. We'll find another way rather than letting it stop the assessment.

Step four: what you get

An explanation
In plain language, in the appointment, with time to ask everything twice if you want to.
A written summary
Yours to keep, to read again later, and to show whoever else needs to see it.
A plan
What happens next, who does it, and when. Including the things that aren't medical.
A letter to your GP
Structured and specific, so your own doctor can act on it straight away.
A review
Memory changes over time. We'll tell you when to come back and what would bring that forward.
Somewhere to call
Questions don't arrive on schedule. You can ring us after the appointment.

The things people actually worry about

What if it turns out to be nothing?

Then you'll know, and you can stop carrying it around. That is a good outcome, not a wasted appointment.

What if it turns out to be something?

Then you've found out sooner, which means more time to plan, more options, and more say in what happens. People tell us the not knowing was the worst part.

Can I fail this?

No. It isn't an exam and there's nothing to revise for. It's a conversation and some structured questions, with a specialist who does this all day.

Does it have to mean dementia?

No. Thyroid problems, medications, depression, hearing loss, vitamin deficiency and sleep can all affect memory, and several of those are fixable.

What will it cost?

Most assessments attract a Medicare rebate. We'll tell you exactly what you'll pay before anything is booked. No surprises, and no obligation to continue.

Do I need a GP referral?

It helps, and it's usually needed for the Medicare rebate. Get in touch first if you'd rather, and we'll talk you through it.

Families and carers

You noticed. That counts.

Most people who reach us were brought here by someone else. A daughter rang. A husband finally said it out loud. A neighbour mentioned it to the GP.

Raising it is the hardest part

Not the appointment. The conversation before it.

Start with what you saw, not what you think it means

"You asked me about Tuesday three times yesterday" lands very differently from "I think something's wrong with your memory."

Pick a calm moment, not a bad one

Straight after a mistake is the worst time. The conversation goes better when nobody is already embarrassed.

Name the fear rather than stepping around it

Most people have already thought about it and are frightened. Saying so out loud is usually a relief rather than a shock.

Offer the ordinary version

"Let's just get it checked, the way you'd get a sore knee looked at." Many causes of memory change are treatable, and that's a true and useful thing to say.

You don't have to win it the first time

Plant it, leave it, come back. Pushing hard on day one often sets things back.

You can ring us first

Before you say anything to them. Our nurses have this conversation most days and will talk it through with you.

What you've seen matters clinically

Diagnosis leans heavily on how someone is managing day to day, and the person themselves often can't see the change, or plays it down without meaning to.

The detail of what altered, when, and what they can still do is some of the most useful information a specialist gets. You're not telling tales. You're part of the assessment.

Useful things to jot down: when you first noticed. What specifically changed. What they managed a year ago that's harder now. Whether it's getting worse steadily or coming and going. Any falls, any new medicines.

You'll be asked to complete a short questionnaire about daily function. Answer it honestly rather than kindly. Kind answers make the picture less clear.

For referring GPs

Your patient, back in your care

We're a specialist assessment service, not a replacement for the relationship you already have. We take the work-up off your plate and send you something you can act on.

What we take on

Case preparation

We collect prior records, correspondence and results, and chase what's missing. Your practice sends the referral and nothing else.

Collateral history

Structured informant history from family, captured properly rather than squeezed into a consultation.

Cognitive assessment

Administered by a clinician, with the instrument chosen for the patient rather than for convenience. Scores returned as data, not a screenshot.

Specialist consultation

A geriatrician or old-age psychiatrist, by video, with a full hour.

The difficult conversation

Diagnosis is disclosed by the specialist, with family present, and with time for it.

A plan you can run

Specific recommendations with who does what, written to be actionable in general practice.

How referrals reach us

Through Medical-Objects, into and out of Best Practice and Medical Director. If you already send specialist referrals electronically, this works the same way.

We write back through the same channel, so the report lands in your inbox rather than in a patient's handbag.

Useful to include: what prompted the referral, current medications, recent bloods, any imaging, and who in the family is involved.

Not required: a completed cognitive screen. If you've done one we'll use it; if you haven't, that's not a reason to delay the referral.

Who we're right for

Well suited: adults with memory or cognitive concerns where a specialist opinion would help, particularly where travelling to a metropolitan memory clinic is the barrier.

Not suited: acute confusion or suspected delirium, acute psychiatric presentations, and anyone who needs to be seen today. Those need your usual urgent pathways, not a booked telehealth assessment.

We begin accepting referrals in mid-2027, on the Gold Coast and in South Brisbane first. We'd still like to hear from you before then: the referral pathway and the report format are being designed with GPs now.

Our clinicians

Who you'll actually see

Not a call centre, and not software. A named specialist who sees you for a full hour and whose name is on the letter that goes back to your GP.

The team around an assessment

A nurse

Your first call and your point of contact throughout. They gather the history, arrange the tests and make sure the technology works before the day.

A geriatrician or old-age psychiatrist

The specialist who assesses you, reaches the diagnosis and explains it. A geriatrician for most people; an old-age psychiatrist where mood, behaviour or psychiatric history is prominent.

A pharmacist, where medications are part of the picture

Polypharmacy is one of the genuinely treatable contributors to memory change in older people, and it is easy to miss in a short appointment.

Your own GP

Not an afterthought. They keep the relationship and the ongoing care, and we write to them properly.

Privacy

What we do with your information

Health information is sensitive, and we treat it that way. Here is what we collect, why, and who sees it.

What we collect

Your name and contact details. What you tell us about memory, thinking or daily life. Health information from your GP and others, where you have agreed to us collecting it. The records of your assessment, including notes, scores and correspondence.

Why we collect it

To assess you, to plan your care, and to write back to your GP. We also keep records because registered health practitioners are legally required to.

Who sees it

The clinicians and staff involved in your care, and your GP where you have agreed. Nobody else, unless you ask us to share it or the law requires it. We do not sell your information and we do not use your health information for marketing.

Two separate consents

Agreeing to be assessed and agreeing to hear from us about the clinic are different things. You can say yes to one and no to the other, and you can change your mind about either at any time without it affecting your care.

Where it is kept

In secure systems hosted in Australia. Access is limited to the people involved in your care, and every time your record is opened it is logged. Some cognitive tests run on platforms operated overseas. Where that applies to you, we will tell you before your assessment and you can decline.

How long we keep it

Clinical records are kept for at least seven years after your last appointment, and for a person under 18 until they turn 25. That is the legal requirement for health records in Australia.

Seeing and correcting your record

You can ask for a copy of what we hold about you, and you can ask us to correct anything that is wrong. Ask any member of our team and we will tell you how.

If you are unhappy

Tell us first and we will look into it. If you are not satisfied with how we handle it, you can complain to the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner at oaic.gov.au.
